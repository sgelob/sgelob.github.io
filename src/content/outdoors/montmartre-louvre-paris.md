Ten kilometres and 55 metres of climbing on foot, from the hill of Montmartre down through the west of Paris to the Louvre: blue sky over the Butte in the late morning, grey clouds by the time I reached the Champs-Élysées.

The walk begins by climbing to the terrace below Sacré-Cœur, where the whole city lies in the haze beyond the lawns. I came down past the Place Suzanne Valadon, where a tile mosaic on the wall spells the name of the city, through the market stalls of the Abbesses and under an archway into the Rue Lepic, which drops over the cobbles to the red windmill of the Moulin Rouge.

Along the Boulevard des Batignolles the white domes of Sacré-Cœur were already far behind. The Avenue Vélasquez, quiet under its plane trees, leads into the Parc Monceau. The Duke of Chartres laid it out as his private garden in the 1770s; the city bought it in 1860 and it reopened in August 1861, the first new public park of Haussmann’s Paris. In November it was all yellow ginkgo and rust-coloured crowns, behind gates of gilded wrought iron.

From the Arc de Triomphe I walked the whole length of the Champs-Élysées, past a grinning cat with a halo drawn on a hoarding, to the Place de la Concorde, where a crowd was waiting behind a tape in front of the colonnades.

The last stretch crosses the Jardin des Tuileries: a round pond with a Ferris wheel reflected in it, a black and white sculpture among the chestnuts, and from the Carrousel the view straight back along the axis, with the obelisk and the Arc de Triomphe in line.
