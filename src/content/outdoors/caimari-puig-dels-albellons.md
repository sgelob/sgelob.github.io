Five and a half kilometres and 397 metres of climbing on foot, a short loop from Caimari up into the first mountains of the Serra de Tramuntana, in the warm light of an August evening.

The way leaves the village through narrow streets of stone houses and climbs past old olive trees behind dry-stone walls, then on stony paths through holm oaks and pines. Higher up the trees open onto the plain of Mallorca far below, with the coast in the haze, and on the top a survey pillar stands among the pines and the grass.

The way down passes a ring of stones in a clearing, crags and an overhanging rock wall, a few cows among the bushes, and comes back to the village by the old road to Lluc, below a sheer limestone wall and terraces of olive trees climbing the hillside.

Caimari lives on its olives. According to the Majorca Daily Bulletin, oil has been made here since Roman times, but the groves only spread widely from the sixteenth century, and above all in the eighteenth and nineteenth, when the dry-stone terraces were built to farm the steep slopes. The same oak woods also fed the village’s charcoal burners.
