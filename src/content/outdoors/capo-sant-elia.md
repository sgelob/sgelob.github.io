Three and a half kilometres and 129 metres of climbing on foot, an early morning loop over Capo Sant’Elia, the headland that closes the bay of Cagliari to the east, in July.

From the cove of Calamosca the path climbs through the scrub of agaves, over bare white rock, with the low sun glittering on the sea and the long beach of Poetto and its lagoon stretching away below. On the far side the limestone drops in white cliffs into water of an unreal turquoise, with small rocky coves at their foot.

Above Calamosca stands an old tower. According to the regional heritage site, it was already built by 1638, when the Spanish were ringing the Sardinian coast with watchtowers, and it was known as the signal tower, because it passed word of the ships to the Castello of Cagliari.

The headland is also called the Sella del Diavolo, the devil’s saddle. The legend told by the Sardinian tourist board has the archangel Michael and his angels fighting Lucifer in the sky above the bay; in the battle Lucifer lost the saddle of his mount, which fell into the sea and became the hill, and the bay was named the Gulf of Angels.
